Questions we get asked

How do you win back lost B2B customers?

Work account by account. Take off anyone you should not contact, check who each account is today, find the real reason it left and look for what has changed since. Put forward only the accounts with a clear reason to call, rehearse the objections, then have the person they will listen to make the call.

Start with who you can contact, and who they are today

Pull every churned account from the last 24 months in your CRM. Then take off anyone who should not be on the list: customers you terminated, accounts suspended for non-payment, live customers trading under another name and anyone else you have chosen to stop working with. A customer you let go, or one still paying you, should never get a win-back call.

Next, check who each account is today. Companies rename, merge and get acquired. The account in your CRM may now be part of a larger group, trading under a new name or closed altogether. A call to the wrong company, or to someone who left two years ago, wastes the one chance you have to make a good first impression the second time round.

Find the real reason they left

The reason in the CRM is only a starting point. Dropdown reasons are picked in a hurry, often by the person who has just lost the account. "Price" is an easy one to pick and often not the full story. If it says price, check their last login. An account that barely used the product did not leave over price.

Read what the account said when it left, from exit emails and support tickets to the notes from the last call, and check it against how they actually used the product. The two often tell different stories. Where you cannot back a reason with what the account said or did, write down that you do not know. A wrong reason leads to the wrong opening line.

Talk to the people who worked the account as well. The account manager, the support lead and whoever ran onboarding will each remember something the CRM missed.

Look for what has changed since

Most accounts leave for a reason that does not last. The person who pushed to drop you leaves, the missing feature ships, the budget resets. The question for each account is whether its reason has expired. There is more on this in why lost B2B customers come back.

Look at what each account has done since it left. Leadership changes, funding, launches, hiring and new offices are all public signals. A company hiring finance roles may now need the invoicing feature it missed. A new head of operations may not share the old one's view of you. Changes on your side count too: a feature you have shipped, a smaller plan or a fix for the problem that drove them away.

Only put an account forward when there is real and recent proof it is worth a call. If nothing has changed, the answer is no for now, and that is worth knowing too.

Prepare the call before anyone picks up the phone

A win-back call is harder than a cold call. The person on the other end remembers what went wrong, and the first objection is usually some version of "why would it be different this time?"

Before calling, write down who the call is to, why they left, what has changed and a single line on why now. Then list the objections they are likely to raise and how you would answer each one. Think about everyone who will have a say, beyond your contact: the finance lead who watches the cost, the operations lead who remembers the rollout and the person who used the product every day.

Open with what has changed. Acknowledge why they left, show that you understood it and ask whether a conversation is worth their time. Leading with an apology or a discount puts the past back at the centre of the call.

Keep the opening short: one sentence on why you are calling, one on what has changed and a question. The rest of the call is listening.

Rank the list and make it someone's job

You will not call every lost account, and you should not. Rank them. Accounts where the reason has clearly expired and the timing is right go first. Accounts with a damaged relationship, or with no sign of change, stay off the list for now.

Then decide who makes each call. Usually that is whoever the account is most likely to pick up for, which may not be the original account owner. And decide whose job lost accounts are. If nobody owns lost accounts, nobody calls them, and the ones worth winning back slip past again.

If you have lost a handful of customers and remember exactly why each one walked, you can do all of this in an afternoon. With a longer list, each step takes research most teams never get to, which is the question we look at in whether win-back research can be done in-house.

Questions on this topic

Which lost customers are worth winning back?

The ones where the reason they left has expired and there is a credible way to open the call. Leave out accounts you were glad to lose, anyone still paying you and relationships that are damaged.

Is it worth trying to win back customers who left?

For accounts where the reason has expired, yes. They already know you, which makes them a warmer conversation than any stranger. The work is in finding which accounts those are.

Should the original account owner make the call?

Not always. Choose whoever the account is most likely to take a call from. Sometimes that is the original owner; sometimes it is a senior leader, or someone new who carries none of the history.
