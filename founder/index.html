Founder

Vikrant Sharma, founder of Intellible

I founded Intellible in Dubai in August 2026, after nine years in operations and customer experience across MENA and India. The work is straightforward to describe and slower to do well: we find which churned accounts are worth winning back, why they left, what has changed and how to open the call.

How I got here

I did not start out in this line of work. In the final year of an English literature degree at Delhi University I set up a small traditional business, and it failed quickly enough to send me looking for a job. I opened LinkedIn, which I had never used before, and that is how I arrived at IWS Event & Entertainment, as a business development manager. I pitched brands for sponsorship and sold kiosk space at wedding exhibitions and trade fairs.

A year or so later, an HR colleague from IWS forwarded my CV to a relative at Zomato. I sat every interview that same day and walked home with the offer before the day was over. I led growth for North-West Delhi, digging through data for segments that could actually grow, and launched a marketing campaign that lifted partner retention.

Then I quit, packed my bags and moved to Dubai looking for something bigger, without a clear picture of what that was. Offers arrived that did not light a fire, so I waited. I have always been better at waiting for the right thing than at settling for less. Uber Eats was the one that did, and it became the biggest break of my career. I joined as an EMEA operations coordinator, got more than 1,000 restaurants live across MENA, managed the vendors around photography, shipping and hardware, and reworked onboarding so it was less error-prone. The best part of that chapter had little to do with onboarding: I met my wife there, a colleague at the time.

When Uber Eats shut down in MENA I was out of a job, in the middle of Covid, for a couple of months. Careem was the next chapter, and the way it started is still my favourite story to tell. I had met the hiring team and followed up, only to be told they had heard I had turned the offer down, which I was hardly in a position to do. It was a human error, and it all worked out. If a mix-up like that could turn into a yes, more things can. I spent three months as a supply associate, timing every stage of a last-mile food delivery, finding the gaps and training delivery captains to close them, while watching Careem absorb the business I had just been part of.

CloudKitchens came from a coffee I did not expect. A former general manager from Uber Eats called one day to check in, and suggested we meet. The company was in strict stealth, so he could not even tell me why. Over that coffee we talked about me joining to build things from the ground up, and I was glad to do the scrappy work with old colleagues again. Before I started I went to India for a short break, and a WhatsApp arrived from a former desk mate at Uber, asking how it was going and what was next. I kept it vague, because stealth is stealth, only to find out at the Christmas party that she was going to be my manager and had been pulling my leg all along. Then the real work: I joined as group operations lead, launched dark kitchens in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Kuwait, and set up onboarding, sales and customer success processes from scratch.

EasyOrder arrived as a LinkedIn message from the person who would become my manager: we were in the same industry, and would I be open to a chat. Of course I said yes. It was a clear match, and I joined as head of operations and customer experience. The slightly tricky part was being remote while the rest of the team sat in Belgium, but they made it work. I launched the company's third product, kiosks, put playbooks and a system in place where there had not been one, and built a merchant health scoring framework. It was the closest I came to this kind of work before Intellible.

Why I started Intellible

After EasyOrder I went into fractional consulting, building AI-assisted workflows for startups. Outreach was the hard part, because I had no strong answer to "what can you do for us?" Every pitch meant first learning what a company did and where it hurt. On the side I started experimenting with machine learning, and learnt XGBoost to predict churn. The model was more accurate than the signal-based one I had built before, and then a doubt arrived that I could not shake. Why would anyone buy a churn predictor that a capable person could build themselves? I wondered whether the same kind of prediction could show which already-churned accounts were worth winning back. I liked the question, and then I did what seemed sensible at the time: I put it aside.

It came back on an ordinary workday. My wife had a meeting in Dubai, I tagged along, and a friend got me into Dubai Founders HQ. I spent the day among young founders who were figuring things out as they went. Nobody had a crystal-clear plan, and the ones who did admitted it never goes to plan. On the drive home I kept replaying a line a friend had quoted from a Y Combinator podcast: it is about productising a solution. The next day I went back to the question I had put aside, and the idea was still there. That became Intellible, a win-back intelligence service for B2B teams. Intellible does this work account by account, and hands the call back to the people who already know the customer.
