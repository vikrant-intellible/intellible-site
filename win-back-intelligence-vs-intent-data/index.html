Questions we get asked

Is intent data enough to know which lost accounts to call?

Intent and signal data can tell you that something changed at a lost account, such as hiring, funding or research on a topic. It cannot tell you why the account really left, whether that reason has expired or how your team should open the call. Win-back intelligence answers those questions and treats signals as one piece of evidence.

What intent and signal data does well

Intent data tracks which companies are reading about a topic, often across many websites, and turns that activity into a score. Signal data watches for events in the open: a new leader, a funding round, a burst of hiring, a new office or a change in the systems a company uses. Both usually arrive as alerts or scores inside the CRM.

For winning new business, that is useful. It tells a sales team which companies might be in the market this quarter and gives a rep a reason to reach out to a stranger.

It can help with lost accounts too. An account that went quiet two years ago and is now hiring a finance team has changed in some way, and a signal is often how you hear about it.

What a signal cannot tell you about a lost account

A signal describes a company from the outside. A lost account comes with history that no outside feed can see, and that history decides whether the call is worth making.

  • Why the account really left: the reason sits in your exit emails, support tickets, usage logs and the notes from the last call. A signal feed has never read any of them.
  • Whether that reason has expired: a funding round matters if the account left over budget. It means far less if the account left after a rollout went badly and the person who ran it is still there.
  • Whether you should call at all: customers you terminated, accounts suspended for non-payment and live customers trading under another name all produce signals as well. None of them should get a win-back call.
  • Who the account is today: companies rename, merge and get acquired. An alert matched to an old name or web address can miss the change, or attach news to the wrong record.
  • How to open the call: an alert names a company and an event. It does not say who will decide, which objections they will raise or what your rep should say in the opening minute.

Why more alerts can make the list harder to work

Point a signal feed at a long list of lost accounts and the alerts pile up quickly. Most companies hire, launch and change leaders all the time, so almost every account looks active in some way.

Without the reason each account left, every alert looks equally important. A rep sees that a former customer has opened a new office and calls, with no idea that the account left after a billing dispute that was never settled. That call can close a door that a better-timed one would have opened.

Topic intent has the same problem. A lost account reading about your category might be looking for a replacement for the supplier it moved to, or checking that it chose well. The score looks the same either way.

The result is a busy feed and a team that still does not know who to call this week.

How win-back intelligence uses signals

Win-back intelligence works the other way round. It starts with the reason each account left, then looks for the change that matters to that reason.

Take an account that left because you had no invoicing API. You have since shipped one, and the account is now hiring finance roles and has opened a second branch. The reason has expired, the timing is right and there is a clear way to open the call. That account goes near the top of the list.

Now take an account that left after a serious failure, which is also hiring. The same signal carries a different meaning, and the right advice may be to leave it alone.

Signals stay in the work as evidence. They are read alongside what the account said when it left and how it actually used what it bought. An account goes forward when there is real and recent proof that it is worth a call, and stays off the list when there is not.

Each account on the finished list comes with the reason it left, whether that reason still holds, what has changed, why now, who to contact and how to open the call. Before anyone calls, the pitch is tested against the people who will decide, so the likely objections already have answers.

When signal data is enough, and when it is not

If you have lost a handful of customers and remember exactly why each one walked, a signal alert plus your own memory may be all you need. When the alert lands, call them.

If the list is long, the reasons vary and the CRM notes are thin, the alerts need something to be read against. That is research, account by account, and it is the part most teams never get to. Questions on this topic

Does a signal alert mean a lost account is ready to talk?

Not by itself, because an alert says something happened at the company and nothing about why the account left. A funding round is a reason to call an account that left over budget, and far less of one for an account that left after a dispute.

Which signals matter most for a lost account?

The ones that bear on the reason it left. Leadership changes, new funding, launches, hiring and new offices all count, and so do changes on your side, such as a feature the account asked for or a plan that now fits it.

Can we keep using our intent data alongside win-back intelligence?

Yes, and the feed becomes more useful once each alert can be read against the reason the account left. Signals are one piece of the evidence we read, along with what the account said on the way out and how it used what it bought.
